White-Label Web Development: How US Agencies Choose a Partner (Checklist)

5 November 2026 · Tech Phase

Short answer: A good white-label web development partner builds under your brand without ever contacting your clients. They work to your process and standards, and they hand over code and files that you and your client own. To choose one, check confidentiality first (NDA, no credits, you stay the point of contact). Then check quality on a paid trial project, and communication and time-zone overlap. Last, check that scope, pricing and change requests are clear in writing.

White-label partnerships fail for predictable reasons: missed deadlines, quality you have to rework, a partner who contacts your client, or arguments about scope. The checklist below is built to catch those before you sign.

What is white-label web development?

It's when another company builds websites, stores or apps that you sell and deliver to your clients under your own brand. Your client deals only with you. The partner works behind the scenes, so there are no credits in the footer, no partner logos in files, and no direct contact unless you choose it.

Agencies use it to:

  • take on more projects without hiring
  • add services they don't do in-house, such as Shopify, custom code or automation
  • cover peaks, holidays and overflow

How does white-label compare with hiring or freelancers?

Hire in-house Freelancers White-label partner
Fixed cost High: salary, benefits and tools None None
Speed to add capacity Slow: weeks to months to hire Fast, but varies Fast, once onboarded
Range of skills Limited to who you hire One skill set per person Usually design, development and QA
Your control Highest Medium Medium to high, with a clear process
Confidentiality Built in Depends on the agreement Should be contractual: NDA and no credits
Main risk Idle cost when work is quiet Availability and reliability Choosing the wrong partner

For a sense of in-house cost, the median US wage for web developers and digital designers was $99,520 a year in May 2025, before benefits and overheads (US Bureau of Labor Statistics).

Will the partner stay invisible to your clients?

Confidentiality is the core of white-label. Check:

  • They'll sign an NDA covering your clients, pricing and processes
  • No partner credits, logos or links in the site, code comments, file names or metadata
  • You stay the only point of contact. They never contact your client unless you ask them to
  • They're happy to work in your tools, such as your project board, your Slack or your email domain, or with no client-facing presence at all
  • Staging sites can run on your domain or a neutral one
  • They won't show your client work in their own portfolio without written permission

Can they match your quality standards?

Don't judge on a portfolio alone. Check:

  • Live sites you can open and test on mobile and with PageSpeed Insights
  • They follow your design files, brand and coding standards, not their own templates
  • They have a QA process covering browsers, devices, forms, accessibility basics and broken links
  • SEO basics at launch: titles, meta descriptions, headings, sitemap and redirects on redesigns
  • They work in the platforms you sell, such as WordPress, Shopify, Webflow or custom code
  • They'll do a small paid trial project before a big commitment

How will you work together day to day?

  • Overlap hours with your team are agreed in writing
  • There's a named project lead and a backup
  • Updates come at a fixed rhythm, such as daily or twice weekly, in writing
  • A response-time promise, for example within one business day
  • Designs before development and a staging link before launch, so you can review before your client sees anything
  • A clear revision policy: how many rounds, and what counts as a change

Who owns the work, and is it secure?

  • IP transfers to you, or your client, on payment, in writing. Under US law, contractor work generally isn't "work made for hire" without specific conditions (US Copyright Office, Circular 30). This isn't legal advice, so check your contracts with a lawyer.
  • Code lives in a repository you control, and they hand over source files, such as design files, fonts, licenses and logins
  • Access is least-privilege (only what the job needs) and removed at the end of the project
  • Client data is handled under your privacy commitments
  • Plugins, themes and stock assets are properly licensed in your or your client's name

Are scope and pricing clear?

  • Every project gets a written scope: pages, features, platform, timeline and exclusions
  • Pricing is a fixed quote per project or a clear package, in your currency
  • Change requests are priced before the work starts
  • Milestone payments are tied to deliverables
  • Post-launch support: bug-fix window, maintenance options and response times

What are the red flags?

  • They want to talk to your client directly "to save time"
  • Their portfolio can't be verified, or every site looks the same
  • No written scope, just an hourly rate and "we'll see"
  • Pressure to pay everything upfront
  • Vague answers on who owns the code
  • Slow replies during the sales process, which only gets worse after you sign

What else do agencies ask about white-label partners?

What does white-label web development mean?

A partner builds websites or apps that your agency sells and delivers under your own brand. Your client deals only with you, and the partner stays invisible, with no credits or direct contact.

How do I know a white-label partner won't contact my clients?

Put it in writing. Use an NDA and a non-solicitation clause, and agree that you are the only point of contact, with no partner credits in any deliverable. A paid trial project shows how they actually behave.

How should white-label web development be priced?

A fixed quote per project from a written scope is the easiest model for agencies to resell, because you know your margin before you sell. Priced change requests and milestone payments keep it predictable.

Who owns the code in a white-label project?

It should be you or your client, transferred on payment in writing. Under US law, contractor work isn't automatically work made for hire, so the agreement should include an IP assignment.

Should I start with a trial project?

Yes. A small paid project is the most reliable way to test quality, communication and deadlines before you trust a partner with a major client.
